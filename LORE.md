# alt-slim-pickins — lore

What working sessions *learned* here — not what they did. Appended by
agents and humans; read back by the project card and `/brief`.

## 2026-08-29 — Claude Opus 5

Slim's grammar splits cleanly in two, and the split is the design opportunity. The line skeleton is genuinely one sentence (indentation + one leader from a closed set + the rest; indented lines are children) and holds everywhere. All the irregularity lives in the tag line. Verified against Slim 5.2.1: (1) attribute values are undelimited Ruby terminating at whitespace, so `a href=1+2` yields href=3 but `a href=1 + 2` silently yields href=1 with `+ 2` as text content — no error; (2) `:` is a second nesting mechanism competing with indentation and colliding with embedded-engine syntax — `li: a Home` works, `li:` with an indented block is a SyntaxError; (3) whitespace control is a suffix sublanguage (`p<`, `p>`, `p<>`, `=<`, `==<>`); (4) escaping is encoded by character doubling (`=` escapes, `==` does not). Keep the skeleton; the tag line is what needs redesigning.

## 2026-08-29 — Claude Opus 5

The design collapsed once we stopped defending inherited syntax. Every sigil we had been carrying (= - | and required parens) existed only to disambiguate Ruby that we ourselves had left undelimited — Ruby already tells "Products" from product.name. Remove the original mistake and the whole punctuation layer evaporates. What survived is one sentence, `word arguments`, indentation for nesting, six argument kinds. The load-bearing move is that HTML elements, components, control flow and text are all *words*: extending the language adds vocabulary, never syntax. The word carries presentation, the argument carries domain — `price .price` means present the subject's price as a price, and the formatting belongs to the word. Two of dan's conventions did more than any syntax we invented: `each product` (iterate products, bind the subject, so `.name` just reads) and letting conditionals dissolve into vocabulary (`empty "Nothing here yet."` names the situation rather than writing the branch). Consequence worth remembering: since `.foo` is not valid Ruby, the Slim-parser-subclassing question we spent the session investigating became moot — we own the grammar. And the risk moved with it. The grammar is one sentence and can barely be wrong now; the vocabulary can be incomplete, and in a language where words are the only construct, every missing word is a wall.

## 2026-08-29 — Claude Opus 5

Cutting the qualifier colon bought more than it cost, and the lesson
generalises: when two constructs fill the same grammatical role, spelling one
of them with punctuation is not a distinction, it is an inconsistency. `each
product` was already bare while `section :products` was not — same role, two
spellings. Dropping the colon left a morphology rule stronger than the thing
removed: **a dot means data, a bare word is language.** Every value carries a
dot (`.name`, `order.number`); every name does not (`products`, `primary`).
One visual cue doing one job, and no Ruby-ism the reader needs prior exposure
to.

Rejected the other half of the proposal — attributes as child lines (`id .id`)
alongside `alt:` modifiers — because it makes attributes sayable two ways,
which is the alias problem. But the deeper answer was that the line should not
exist at all: a card whose subject is a product can infer its own id. That is
now a rule in DESIGN.md — *a line which states the inferable should not
exist* — and it is the same cut as dropping `as product` earlier.

Also learned to keep the grammar checker in the repo. It caught four lines of
prose annotation masquerading as grammar inside fenced blocks. A design doc
drifts exactly where its examples stop being checkable.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

The vocabulary entry format is not a matter of taste — it falls out of the
rule of government. Because a name is interpreted by the word to its left and
a word by the word it is nested under, every word needs *two* definitions, one
per axis. Miss either and a sentence using that word cannot be read in
isolation, which is the property the whole grammar exists to protect.

A convention for the docs, learned the practical way: untagged fences hold
sentences and are checked by `check_grammar.rb`; tagging a fence (```` ```html ````)
excludes it. That is how a document shows *output* without the checker
mistaking it for grammar — and it means every example in every doc is either
verified or explicitly marked as not-a-sentence. There is no third category,
which is what stops examples from rotting.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Drafting the vocabulary against a real page (roth/views/controls.slim) rather than invented examples paid for itself three times, and the argument generalises: invented examples are the exact condition under which a vocabulary looks more complete than it is, because you only invent examples for words you already have. (1) Three words the estimate missed entirely — metric, chart, disclosure — each of them hand-built in that file out of markup, classes and JavaScript; the disclosure toggle alone costs a button, an id, a data attribute, a CSS class and a handler. (2) `price` was exposed as a domain noun masquerading as a presentation word. Our own rule says the word carries presentation and the argument carries domain, so it had to become `money`; a portfolio has balances, and `price .balance` is a category error that a products example would never have revealed. (3) Of two predicted collapses, heading/title held but grid/list did not — they govern their children differently (grid lays out blocks, list makes children into items), and government is the test, not superficial similarity. Also: doctype turned out not to be an exception at all, just something `page` knows, which closed the last hole in the grammar. Most valuable mechanical lesson: make the documents accountable to each other. check_grammar.rb now requires every word used in a sentence to be defined and every defined word to have a sentence, and it caught real drift on its first run.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Writing a whole real page is a different instrument from drafting a vocabulary, and it found what drafting could not. Three walls, and the most important one was invisible until collections nested: `table holdings` inside `each account` resolved to a page-level holdings rather than that account's, because the rule said a name is a local else a helper. Silently wrong, and in the ordinary shape of every page rather than an edge case. The rule is now subject-first: a name resolves against the subject, then locals, then helpers. It costs nothing at the top level and it retroactively explains `metric balance`, which had only ever worked on instinct. Second wall: no word for navigation — predicted as under-tested in draft 1, bit on the first real page. Third wall taught the better lesson: flash messages had nowhere to live, and adding a `flash` word was the obvious move and the wrong one. If every page would carry an identical line, that is the signature of an *inference*, not a word — so `page` infers it. That is a reusable test for whether something belongs in the vocabulary at all. Also worth remembering: two quantitative claims written into the draft (sentence count, comparison line count) were both wrong when measured. Counts asserted from memory while writing prose are unreliable even about files you just read — measure them, and the honest ratio (66 sentences against 91 lines) is less flattering and more useful than the one imagined. This language does not win by being terser line-for-line; it wins where the old page pays in kind.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

The content-heavy page (modelled on dashboard/views/pattern.slim) hit six walls to the portfolio's three, and the asymmetry was predictable: draft 1 was written against a dashboard, so its blind spot was exactly prose and media. Lesson — a vocabulary drafted against one kind of page is complete-looking in proportion to how much that page resembles the drafting source. Two findings worth keeping. First, `prose` disposes of escaping as a language feature. It is the one word that renders markup and it sanitises rather than trusts, so there is no sigil for "trust me" and nothing like Slim's = versus ==. A whole class of syntax vanished because one word owns the capability. Second, and more important, the sixth wall was answered with "no" rather than a word: pattern.slim composes a sentence from a text fragment plus a link, and our grammar cannot, because one line is one node. Adding inline fragments would have put a second composition mechanism beside indentation — Slim's `:` all over again, the exact mistake this language exists to avoid. Rich inline text is markdown's job. Knowing which walls to refuse is as much a part of vocabulary work as knowing which to fill. Also: `list` was wrong (draft 1 said each child becomes an item, which breaks as soon as an item holds two things) and survived the portfolio page only because list never appeared there — untested entries rot silently. And a recurring personal failure mode: three quantitative claims asserted before measuring, all three wrong, second round running. Measure before writing the sentence, not after.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Two naming lessons and a convergence signal. (1) `detail` was a misnomer nobody would have caught from inside the vocabulary: HTML's <details> is the disclosure element, and we already had `disclosure` for it, so a reader who knows HTML would have read `detail` as its singular and been wrong. Renamed to `fact`. The general rule — when naming a presentation word, check it against the HTML element of the same name, because our readers know HTML even though our vocabulary deliberately does not mirror it. (2) The tempting generalisation — one markup word with markdown/mathml/musicxml/code as variants — is half right and the half matters. Markdown, asciidoc and plain all produce a block of prose, so they are variants of one word. MathML, MusicXML and source code are three different presentations, and our rule is that the word carries the presentation. Grouping them would organise the vocabulary by *mechanism* (things passed through a renderer), which is the one axis this vocabulary must never use, and the immediate proof is that `prose code, …` would alias `snippet`. Mechanism-grouping is the subtlest way this design can rot, because it always looks like elegant generalisation. (3) Wall counts across three real pages: 3, then 6, then 1. image and icon needed no changes on first contact. Converging rather than growing — which is the first real evidence the vocabulary is approaching complete. Also, three pages have now failed to reach the `?` hole (a bare helper used as a value); every condition in real use was about the subject and carried a dot. Three misses is enough to decide it deliberately rather than keep listing it.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

VBA's implied Application object closed the last hole in the grammar, and the shape of the fix is the lesson. The `?` case — a bare helper used as a value, `when signed_in?` — looked like a gap in the morphology for four rounds. It was not. It was a missing object at the bottom of the subject chain. The chain could run out: at the top level there was no subject, so `.foo` had no referent. Give the chain a floor — the page itself, whose attributes are the locals and helpers — and `when .signed_in?` is ordinary, read by the same dot that reads an account's balance. Resolution went from two rules to one, because "then a local, else a helper" is no longer a fallback; locals and helpers *are* the page's attributes. The diagnostic worth remembering: three real pages never reached the hole, and that was the clue rather than reassurance. Pages had been quietly routing around the absence by phrasing every condition against a subject. When repeated real use never reaches a supposed gap, suspect the gap is misdiagnosed — something is missing one level down from where you are looking. Fourth time in this project the right fix was deleting a special case rather than adding syntax (after `as product`, `id .id`, and the qualifier colon). The ambient object is never written, so it costs no syntax and needs no word; the honest price is that `.foo` at top level has an invisible referent, paid once in one paragraph.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Two lessons, one about roadmaps and one about trusting your own tools. (1) The phase order fell out of a risk register rather than a dependency graph, and that inverted what looked like the obvious first step. Forty-four of forty-six words infer something and thirteen infer from the app's domain model, so inference is the entire bet and has no evidence; the transform is ~150 lines of a thing we are confident about. Building the transform first would have felt like progress while retiring no risk — it completes part of one item in Phase 0. Phase 0 is therefore a vertical slice (transform + runtime + inference, ten words, rendering the roth form) because roth/views/controls.slim already exists to diff against, and `field` is the heaviest inference in the vocabulary. Two High risks retired by one small slice. (2) check_grammar.rb had been printing its findings and exiting 0 for the whole session. Every "0 problems" was truthful, but the green was never *enforceable* — it could not have gated anything, and I had been reporting it as if it could. It was also scanning fences with a regex, which cannot pair them: a closing ``` is indistinguishable from an opening one, so it read prose between two tagged blocks as grammar. Both defects only surfaced when a document with mixed tagged and untagged fences was added. The general lesson: a verification tool that has never failed has never been verified, and one that grew by six rounds of in-place patches while quietly becoming the project's only evidence deserves a rewrite rather than a seventh patch.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Reuse was a High risk the roadmap's first draft missed entirely, and the reason it was missable is the lesson. Every other risk in the register would announce itself as a wall — a page you cannot write. This one announces itself as pages that work and are tedious, which is the failure mode hardest to notice from inside, because nothing ever fails. dan named it: Sinatra and Slim already have layouts and partials, so a language that cannot match them is a regression however good the grammar is. Worth generalising: when auditing a design, ask separately what would *fail* and what would merely be *worse than the incumbent*. The second class never surfaces from testing alone. The evidence turned out to be sitting in the three drafted pages and was measurable rather than a matter of taste: stylesheet, nav and footer appear at page level in all three — identical in shape, different in content, which is precisely a template with holes and therefore a layout. The list → each → item shape recurs three times across two pages; grid metrics plus several metric lines recurs twice. Both abstractions are earned by repetition already committed, not anticipated. The candidate design keeps the founding principle literally true: if extending the language adds vocabulary and never syntax, a partial is simply *a word an app defines*, invoked like a built-in and taking the current subject as its own. slim-pickins owns the vocabulary of presentation; an app owns the vocabulary of its own components; a reader cannot tell from the call site which is which. Shadowing a built-in must be an error rather than an override — two meanings for one word is the alias problem wearing a new hat.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Phase 0 answered the only question that mattered, and the answer was three-quarters yes. Inference works: across nine fields shared with the hand-written roth page, type and value agreed exactly every time, all derived rather than stated. But `field` claimed four derivations from one word and reliably delivers three. Input name, value and type are mechanical. The label is a judgement call, and humanising got roughly three in ten — `trad_balance` is not "Traditional Balance", `ss_primary_start_year` is not "SS Start (yrs from now)". No cleverer humaniser fixes this: the information is simply not in the attribute name. The general lesson is about what inference can and cannot reach — mechanical facts derivable from a value's *shape* are free; facts that encode human judgement about a domain are not, and a language should not promise them. The strongest and most surprising result is that the app contract is nearly free, because the input type comes from the *value's class* rather than from a schema. Engine::Inputs is a bare Struct with keyword_init and needed no changes at all. Design your inference off runtime values, not off declarations, and plain Ruby objects satisfy you without ceremony. Two things fell out rather than being designed: names compile to Symbols and content to Strings, so words distinguish them by type instead of by counting positions; and `page scenario` followed by a bare `form` is correct, because naming the same subject twice states the inferable — a word that names no subject must leave the chain alone. Finally, diffing against a real hand-written page found a bug in roth itself: its form posts social_security_* while the model has ss_primary_* and ss_spouse_*, so two fields post names nothing reads and two model fields have no inputs. Reading fields from the model makes that class of drift impossible, which is an argument for the language that no amount of design discussion would have produced.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Phase 1: the app contract is one sentence, and the way we got it matters. Rather than write down what an app *ought* to provide, I audited every call the runtime actually makes on an app object. That reduced the required half to: a subject answers the attributes it is asked for, by ordinary method call. No base class, no module, no schema. The audit also found Subject#attributes was speculative dead code that had never been called — writing a contract from the code rather than from intention is what exposed it. The optional half is one method, label_for, and it exists because Phase 0 proved humanising cannot recover acronyms or domain phrasing. Precedence: page, then app, then language — each level owned by whoever knows most. Measured: the roth form has fourteen labelled controls, humanising alone needed eleven written into the page, and a twelve-line LABELS hash on the app took that to zero. Two error-quality lessons worth generalising. A nil subject used to report "this account has no name" — true and useless, because the failure is that the subject is empty, not that it lacks an attribute; different failures need different sentences or they send readers to the wrong place. And more importantly: `page account` with no account used to skip quietly, so the error surfaced three lines later on a line that was not the cause. Quietly tolerating a missing thing always relocates the error away from its origin. A word that names no subject leaves the chain alone; a word that names one that is not there must fail on its own line.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Phase 2 produced the projects first real *rule*, because the same finding happened twice. Phase 0: a value shape gives you an input type but not a label. Phase 2: a value shape gives you Numeric but not money-versus-count-versus-rate. Both were solved identically, by asking the app (label_for, then format_for) with three levels of precedence — page, app, language. Stated generally: mechanical facts derivable from a value shape are free; facts encoding a human judgement about the domain belong to the app, and the language should ask rather than guess. A number is right-aligned unasked because alignment follows from being a number; whether it is money follows from nothing the value knows. Two vocabulary corrections, both forced by the first render rather than by argument. `section` names a *topic*, not a subject — draft 3 said subject and `section summary` immediately asked a portfolio for a summary it lacks; counted across the drafted pages, six of ten sections name a topic with no such attribute anywhere. A topic is a label, not a claim, so section shifts the subject only when the subject has it while page/form/table/each still require what they name. And `each` must iterate the subject when the subject *is* the collection, because `section accounts` shifting to the collection is exactly what lets `empty` know what is empty. Methodological note worth keeping: the tests found a bug the demos structurally could not. Subject#fetch understood hash keys and respond_to? did not, so hash-backed subjects diverged from struct-backed ones — and every demo used Structs. Cheap fixtures in tests are not a lesser version of real fixtures; they cover different ground.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

dan asked whether a word could take a subject *or* just a label, and it exposed a fix I had shipped as worse than I described it. Phase 2 made `section` shift the subject only when the subject happened to have the named attribute. That is opportunism, and opportunism on *runtime data* is the worst kind of context-sensitivity: the meaning of a page depends on something no reader of the page can see. Worse, it was a latent trap — adding an `allocation` attribute to a model would silently make an untouched page's `section allocation` start shifting the subject, so every `.foo` beneath it resolves somewhere new. Exactly the failure Phase 1 taught us to refuse (quiet tolerance relocating an error from its origin), wearing different clothes, and I did not recognise it because it looked like leniency rather than silence. The answer was already in the grammar and I had not looked: a *name* and *content* are different argument kinds with different spellings, so `section accounts` (subject shifts, must exist) and `section "Where you stand"` (heading, shifts nothing) are simply two different sentences. Which job a word is doing became visible in the page. Generalised at grammar level rather than patched onto one word: a word's name slot either takes a subject or it does not, fixed per word, never decided at runtime. Lesson to carry: when a word seems to need two behaviours, check whether the grammar already has two spellings before inventing a runtime rule to choose between them.

## 2026-08-30 — Claude Opus 5

Phase 3 tested the founding claim — extending the language adds vocabulary, never syntax — against the feature most likely to need an exception, and it held. Reuse cost exactly one word, `contents`. A layout is chrome with a hole; a partial is a word an app defines, written in the language, taking the current subject. No page mentions the layout, because `page` infers it — the same rule that disposed of `flash`: an identical line on every page is the signature of an inference. The layout exposed something worth more than the layout itself. A layout renders inside the body but a stylesheet belongs in the head. Rather than restricting where a layout may speak, `page` now assembles the document after its children run, and head-bound words write to the head from any depth. Generalised: a word says where it belongs, not where it is written — and meta, stylesheet and script all got it free. Second generalisable finding: depth is a property of where a word *runs*, not where it is written. `title` inside the account_card partial renders h3 in a loop under a section and h2 on a detail page, from identical source. That is precisely what makes a partial reusable rather than a copy. And a prediction that missed: the roadmap expected the repeated list/item shape to collapse into a partial. Once pages actually ran, the real repetition was the account card. Repetition predicted from reading is not the repetition you find from running — the same lesson as drafting the vocabulary against a real page rather than an imagined one.
